Getting promoted

Promotion case example: consultant to manager, filled in

Most advice tells you what a promotion case should include. This shows you what a finished one actually looks like, part by part, using the structure from my Promotion Case Template.

About this example: Priya is an illustrative composite, not a real person. She's built from the kind of case a Big 4 consultant going for manager typically makes, so the numbers are realistic, not real. Swap in your own evidence, because the panel wants yours.

I'm Kajol Phadnis. I wrote a promotion case every time I went up at Deloitte, and the difference between a case that lands and one that doesn't is rarely the person. It's whether the evidence is easy to see. Here's what that looks like on the page.

Meet Priya

Priya is a consultant in a customer strategy practice at a Big 4 firm, four years in, working mostly on retail and consumer clients. She's going for Manager. Her firm assesses manager candidates on four things: client leadership and delivery, people leadership, business development, and commercial acumen.

1. The headline

One sentence at the top, written as a statement, not a request:

“I’m ready for Manager because I’ve run the loyalty workstream end to end for 7 months, with my Partner supporting my case.”

It names the level, gives the single strongest proof and says who backs her, in under 25 words. It follows the headline formula I use in every case: “I'm ready for [next level] because I'm already operating at it — [one line of the single strongest proof].”

2. Criteria, mapped to evidence

Each criterion gets one piece of evidence, ideally with a number:

CriterionPriya's evidence
Client leadership & deliveryRan the grocery loyalty relaunch workstream day to day for 7 months
People leadershipLed a team of 4 (2 analysts, 2 contractors); coaches the graduate intake
Business developmentCo-wrote the proposal that won a £1.2m follow-on
Commercial acumenHer development area: shadowing bid pricing now (see part 6)

Notice the last row. She doesn't pretend. A weak criterion, named and with a plan, reads as self-aware. Hidden, it reads as a gap the panel found for her.

3. Impact, in numbers

  • £2.1m revenue influenced across the two programmes she worked on this year
  • £1.2m follow-on won, from a proposal she co-wrote
  • A reusable diagnostic toolkit she built, picked up by two later projects

Every line says what changed because of her. None of them says “worked on” or “supported”.

4. Proof she already works a level up

The strongest part of Priya's case is time. She's been doing the manager's job on the loyalty workstream for seven months: running the plan, preparing the steering committee and managing the team. That makes it a pattern, not a one-off, which is exactly what a panel wants to see.

5. Visibility and who backs her

Her Partner is sponsoring the case, and she's made sure the Retail practice lead knows her work. She also has short written quotes ready from the client and from someone she's managed, because named feedback carries more weight than her own description of herself.

6. The gaps, handled honestly

Commercials: pricing and margin. Her plan: shadow the pricing on the next bid with the engagement manager, and finish a finance course by December. One line each for the gap and the plan.

Why this case works

  • The headline does the work. A panel member who reads only the first line already knows the claim and the proof.
  • Every criterion has evidence. Including the weak one, with a plan attached.
  • It's short. A handful of slides. Panels skim, so the numbers sit where they'll be seen.

Questions people ask about this

What should a promotion case include?

Seven parts: a one-line headline claim, your evidence mapped against the promotion criteria, your impact in numbers, proof you already work at the next level, who backs you, the gaps you're closing, and the ask with a 90-day plan.

How long should a promotion case be?

Short. A summary deck of four or five slides, or a one-to-two page document, is enough. Panels skim, so lead with the headline and put the numbers where they can see them.

Can I copy this example?

Use it to see what good looks like, then write your own: the panel wants your evidence, not Priya's. If you'd rather not build the slides from scratch, the Promotion Case Template has both formats ready to fill in.
